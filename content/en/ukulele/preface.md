# Preface: The First Musical Key for Busy Adults

Have you ever come home late from work, watched a video of someone playing and singing for themselves on a streaming platform, and had a thought pass quietly through your mind: "If only I could sit in my living room with an instrument in my arms and play a song I like—how good would that be?"

Most of the time, though, the next second puts that thought out. Maybe you bought an acoustic guitar once, and on the third day of practising the F major barre chord your left-hand fingers hurt too much to type, so you shelved it. Maybe you downloaded staff notation and tried to teach yourself piano, got lost among the dense rows of tadpole-shaped notes and the treble and bass clefs, and in the end never played a single pop song all the way through.

That is exactly why you have opened this book.

## Why is the ukulele the best starting point for adults who want to sing and play?

Of all the instruments people accompany themselves on, the ukulele has an approachability that is almost cheating. It comes from Hawaii at the end of the nineteenth century, and its name means "jumping flea" in Hawaiian—a picture of how a player's fingers hop lightly across the fretboard. For a busy office worker or a university student today, this small instrument with only four nylon strings has three advantages nothing else replaces:

1. **A very low threshold for finger pain.** Compared with the taut steel strings of an acoustic guitar, a ukulele uses soft nylon or fluorocarbon strings, with low action and low tension. You do not have to go through weeks of blistered fingertips and calluses before your hand settles. On the first day you can already press a clean, bright note with ease.
2. **One finger is enough for a whole chord.** Fretting a C major chord on an acoustic guitar takes three fingers reaching across several frets. On a ukulele you put **one finger, the ring finger**, on the third fret of the first string, and a bright, complete C major chord comes out at once. That means the time from zero to your first pop song sung over your own playing is counted in hours, not months.
3. **It belongs in your everyday life.** It is light enough to leave beside the cushions on the sofa, to push into a suitcase, even to carry to a campsite. You do not need to set aside a room of its own for practice; fifteen minutes before you settle down to watch your show is all it takes to get the plain comfort of music.

| What you are comparing | Ukulele | Acoustic guitar | Digital piano / electric piano |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **How the strings or keys feel** | Nylon or fluorocarbon strings, low tension, almost no finger pain | Steel strings at high tension; sore fingers and blisters at the start | Keys do not hurt, but both hands have to work independently |
| **Difficulty of the first chords** | Very low (many common chords take only 1–2 fingers) | Medium (basic open chords take 3–4 fingers) | Medium (left-hand harmony and right-hand notes at the same time) |
| **Sense of achievement from the first song** | **Very fast (a first song within about 1–3 days)** | Slower (about 2–4 weeks to settle the basic hand shape) | Moderate (about 1–2 weeks for a simple accompaniment) |
| **Carrying and storing it** | About 300–700 g depending on size; carry it in one hand | Bulky (about a 40–41 inch body); needs storage space | Large and heavy; it stays indoors in one place |
| **The styles it suits best** | Mandopop, folk, light acoustic songs, reggae, City Pop | Rock, hard-driving folk, blues ballads, fingerstyle | Classical, modern ballads, jazz, film scores |

## Who is this book for, and what makes it different?

Many ukulele methods on the market assume their reader is a primary-school child, and spend the first ten chapters on 〈小星星〉 ("Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star"), 〈兩隻老虎〉 ("Two Tigers") or painful classical sight-reading from the staff. For a young or adult learner who wants to play Mayday, Deserts Chang, Jay Chou, Accusefive or WeiBird, that does not just fail to resonate—it quickly wears away the precious enthusiasm they came in with.

From the day it was planned, this book settled on **three principles: drop the nursery material, aim at real achievement, and put pop chord progressions first**:

* **No memorising theory you will not use.** We do not throw a pile of staff notation and key-signature formulas at you in chapter one. Every piece of theory here exists to answer a real problem: "How should I strum this song so it sounds good?" "The original is too high—what do I do when I cannot reach it?"
* **You sing a pop song by chapter 5.** The first four chapters spend only the leanest possible space on getting your posture right and teaching you to read a chart. Then chapter 5 goes straight to the all-purpose four chords, the most widely used progression in Mandopop, and you play and sing a hit you already know.
* **KTV-level transposition, and a practical arranger's way of thinking.** From the folk-rock strum and the chunk-and-slap groove, through the colour of City Pop seventh chords, to the capo used in practice and fingerstyle solos, this book takes you step by step from a plain accompaniment machine to a player with a style of your own.

## How to use this book: your 30-day practice map

The worst habit in learning an instrument is three hours in one go at the weekend and then nothing at all for five weekdays. Muscle memory in the brain and the fingers is built by frequent, short bursts of repetition. Set aside just **15 to 20 minutes** a day, and work through all 25 chapters of this book along the four-stage map below:

| Stage | Chapters | Suggested days | The ability you gain | What you should be able to do |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stage 1: A warm-up with no pain** | Chapters 1–4 | Days 1–3 | The parts of the instrument, accurate tuning, a correct hand shape, reading chord diagrams and tab | Tune the ukulele in 30 seconds, and keep a steady downstroke with the metronome |
| **Stage 2: Singing out on pop songs** | Chapters 5–9 | Days 4–14 | The core chords of C major, the folk strum, the ballad arpeggio, triple time and the chunk | Sing and play 3–5 classic Mandopop songs smoothly |
| **Stage 3: Groove and transposition** | Chapters 10–19 | Days 15–24 | Slow soul rhythm, barre chords, the G and F major families, the capo and seventh chords | Take any guitar or ukulele chord chart you find online and move it into a key that suits you |
| **Stage 4: Fingerstyle and beyond** | Chapters 20–25 | Days 25–30 | Single-note and two-note harmony, fingerstyle solos, harmonics and body percussion, the Low G conversion and recording | Play a fingerstyle piece with melody and accompaniment together, and record it |

## Before you turn to page one

Put down the labels adults pin on themselves most often: "I have no musical sense", "my fingers are too short", "I am tone-deaf". Music was never the property of a few geniuses. It is closer to an everyday language—you do not have to become a writer before you can speak; you only need a few of the most useful words and sentence patterns, and you can start saying what you feel.

Now take your ukulele out of the gig bag, take a deep breath, and let us begin by getting to know the new friend who is about to sit with you through countless nights.
