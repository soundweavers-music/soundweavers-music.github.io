# Lesson 7: Recording, Part 2: Latency Compensation and Choosing Between Takes

## Why the recorded sound runs "half a beat behind"

You sing along with the backing track in your headphones, the sound goes into the microphone, through the buffer and onto disk, and that path itself has latency (usually 10–30 ms). The result: **you sang in time, but the recorded waveform sits a little late**.

The software automatically subtracts the latency it knows about (the status bar shows "record latency compensation xx ms"). If the recording **still** lands late, press the "Fix" link beside it and raise the extra compensation:

- Every recording lands **late** → make the number bigger.
- Every recording lands **early** → make the number smaller.

Bluetooth headphones often add more than 150 ms, and not consistently, so **always use wired headphones when recording**.

## Multiple takes

Singing the same passage five times, each with its own good points, is normal in professional recording. The method:

1. Put the playhead where you want to record, press R to arm → record.
2. **Recording does not loop** (the engine forces the loop off); press stop when you have the length you want.
3. If you record again, it **overwrites** the old clip over the same stretch of time (the overlapping part gets trimmed), and it does not stack up several takes automatically. To pick the best phrases, move each finished clip somewhere else first, or record in sections.
3. When you're done, use the scissors (Ctrl+T) to piece together "this line from the second take, that one from the fourth".

This process of "picking the best fragments from several takes and assembling one perfect pass" is called **comping**, and it is the most time-consuming and most effective step in vocal production.

## How it works: microphone position and distance

- 15–20 cm is the usual distance for a vocal; too close and you get the **proximity effect** (more low end, a fatter sound), too far and the room's reflections come in.
- Facing the microphone **slightly off to one side** (15–30 degrees) reduces plosive breath noise.
- Don't let your body drift back and forth while recording — change the distance and the timbre changes with it.

## Try it now

Record the same line three times, each time moving the finished clip down to an empty track or to a free space beside it, then piece the best part of all three into one line. Compare the joins for a "click" (if they aren't aligned, there will be one).
