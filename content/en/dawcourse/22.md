# Lesson 22: The Arranger III: Six Parts, Instruments and Volume

## What each of the six parts does

The arranger always has six parts, and each part is its own MIDI track:

| Part | Job | Without it |
|---|---|---|
| Drums | Timekeeping and the feel of the sections | The whole thing loosens up and you cannot hear the bars |
| Bass | The chord roots, lined up with the kick | The low end feels empty and the chords sound uncertain |
| Piano | The backbone of the chords (arpeggios or block chords) | The chords "cannot be heard" |
| Guitar | Strumming or broken chords, adding mid-range body | The chorus is not full enough |
| Strings | Long notes as a bed, holding the sections up | The chorus is not "big" enough |
| Arpeggio | A flowing line in the upper register | Loses the sparkle (optional) |

## Changing instruments

Each part can pick its instrument on its own. The site has three .sf2 files plus the built-in synth sounds:

- **The original synth sounds**: the lowest latency, good for tweaking while you play
- **Salamander Piano**: real samples; use it for grand piano and electric piano
- **Sonatina Strings**: real samples; use it for strings and string sections

Swap the parts' instruments and the character of the whole song changes: with the same chords, the piano switched to electric piano and the strings to a string section, a ballad turns into City Pop.

## Volume and clipping

- Each part has its own fader (dB). **Balance first, then set the overall volume** — do not touch the master at the start.
- Push past 0 dB and it clips (the waveform flattens and sounds harsh). To get louder you raise everything together, not one track on its own.
- "Clipping" here means **digital clipping**, which is not the same thing as a distortion pedal on an electric guitar.

## How it works: why the bass lines up with the kick

The ear finds it hard to tell where low-frequency energy comes from. If the bass accents and the kick fall apart, the two low sounds pile up and **cancel or double each other**, so one moment it is loud and the next it has vanished (the trade calls this "the bass fighting the kick"). That is why the arranger's bass patterns are written **to the kick's rhythm points**: where the kick lands, the bass changes root in the same place.

## Try it now

In the chorus, **switch each of the four parts off and back on one at a time**, and listen for which one is missed most. You will usually find drums and bass are the critical ones — which is also why mixing starts with the low end.
